# State Tax Conformity Matrix

## Overview

This document provides a comprehensive matrix of how each state conforms to the federal Internal Revenue Code (IRC) for individual income tax purposes. Understanding state conformity is crucial for multi-state tax calculations because it affects how states define taxable income, which deductions they allow, and how quickly they adopt federal tax law changes.

## Key Concepts

### Conformity Types

**Rolling Conformity**: States automatically adopt federal tax law changes as they occur unless explicitly opting out. This means state tax law stays current with federal changes without requiring legislative action.

**Static/Fixed Conformity**: States conform to the IRC as it existed on a specific date. Any federal changes after that date do not apply unless the state legislature updates the conformity date.

**Selective Conformity**: States adopt federal provisions on a case-by-case basis, requiring specific legislative action for each provision.

### Starting Points

**Federal Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)**: The most common starting point (31 states + D.C.). States begin with federal AGI from Form 1040 Line 11, then apply state-specific additions and subtractions.

**Federal Taxable Income**: States begin with federal taxable income (AGI minus standard or itemized deductions), then apply state modifications.

**State Definition**: States define their own income base, though they may reference federal definitions for specific items.

## States Without Income Tax (9 States)

The following nine states do not impose a state income tax on wages:

1. **Alaska** - Relies on oil and gas royalties; no individual income tax
2. **Florida** - State constitution bans personal income tax
3. **Nevada** - No individual or corporate income tax
4. **New Hampshire** - Repealed interest and dividends tax in 2025
5. **South Dakota** - No individual, corporate, estate, or inheritance tax
6. **Tennessee** - No income tax on wages
7. **Texas** - No income, inheritance, or estate tax
8. **Washington** - No personal income tax (capital gains tax applies to high-income investors)
9. **Wyoming** - No individual, corporate, inheritance, or estate tax

## Conformity Trends and Impact

### 2025 Federal Tax Changes (OBBBA/HR 1)

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) passed in 2025 created significant challenges for state tax systems:

1. **Rolling Conformity States**: Automatically adopted changes unless they explicitly decoupled, leading to substantial revenue losses
2. **Static Conformity States**: Protected from automatic adoption but faced decisions about updating conformity dates
3. **Selective Conformity States**: Could adopt favorable provisions while rejecting revenue-reducing changes

### Common State Decoupling Provisions (2025)

Many states decoupled from specific federal provisions:
- **Research & Experimentation (R&E) Expenses**: Alabama, California, Delaware, D.C., Illinois, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island
- **Bonus Depreciation**: California, Delaware, D.C., Illinois, Michigan
- **No Tax on Tips**: D.C., Rhode Island (Michigan allows through 2029)
- **No Tax on Overtime**: D.C., Rhode Island (Michigan allows through 2029)
- **Interest Deductions (IRC 163(j))**: D.C., Michigan, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island

### Revenue Impact Examples

- **Colorado**: $1.2 billion shortfall from rolling conformity
- **Delaware**: $410 million losses over three years
- **Illinois**: $267 million deficit in fiscal year 2025

### Future Implementation Considerations

When implementing additional states:

1. **Check Conformity Type**: Determines whether state automatically adopts federal changes
2. **Identify Starting Point**: Federal AGI, federal taxable income, or state definition
3. **Document Deviations**: State-specific additions and subtractions
4. **Update Conformity Dates**: Static conformity states may update dates annually
5. **Track Legislative Changes**: States frequently modify conformity status
